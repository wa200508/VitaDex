# VitaDex organism art direction

Status: target visual direction. The current app can keep its vector placeholders
while the core discovery and learning features are developed. Final artwork is
not a blocker for that work.

## The illustration teaches the description

Choose the pose, viewpoint, framing, and relevant details from the facts in the
card's introduction. A generic attractive picture is insufficient if it hides the
feature being discussed. Compose the illustration and the introduction together.

Examples:

- A giraffe introduction about its long neck calls for a side or three-quarter
  view showing the neck's full length in relation to the body. Avoid a tightly
  cropped face or a foreshortened view that hides the length.
- Dog: emphasize the nose exploring a familiar environment and readable body
  posture. A single dog illustrates an example, not every breed or size.
- Cat: show fine whiskers clearly against a contrasting background, with natural
  fur and muzzle anatomy. Do not hide the whiskers behind a frontal silhouette.
- Red maple: show recognizable lobed leaves and paired winged seeds at a useful
  scale. If the description discusses autumn color, show it without implying
  every leaf is red year-round. Avoid substituting another maple's leaf shape.
- Kentucky bluegrass: show narrow green leaves and, if underground spreading is
  illustrated, a clearly understandable cutaway. Check rhizomes and roots against
  botanical references; do not turn thin stems into oversized woody connections.
- American robin: expose the reddish-brown chest in a natural lawn-foraging pose.
  Use American robin proportions and markings, not a European robin's appearance.

These examples guide composition. The animal's activity and every illustrated
biological feature must remain consistent with the card's cited facts.

## Visual treatment

Aim for realistic, recognizable organisms with a restrained anime finish: natural
proportions, credible anatomy, clear contours, simplified color regions, and soft
shading. The intended effect resembles smoothing a real photograph with a
bilateral filter while preserving important edges and biological details.

Avoid chibi proportions, exaggerated eyes, fantasy anatomy, flat geometric icons,
and decorative effects that obscure the organism. Keep natural coloration and
use calm, softly simplified backgrounds so the teaching features remain prominent.
Images should invite a young child to look closely without misrepresenting the
organism as a toy or implying that it is safe to approach.

## Production and review

1. Start with the card's introduction and its source-supported visual traits.
2. Write an art brief naming the traits to emphasize, viewpoint, intended pose,
   crop-safe area, anatomical constraints, and what must not be implied.
3. Consult trustworthy reference imagery. If a photograph is copied, transformed,
   or used in an adaptation, check its license and keep attribution and provenance.
   Do not apply a filter to an arbitrary web photograph and assume it is reusable.
4. Review anatomy, markings, seasonality, and species-specific features separately
   from aesthetic quality. Generated art is not scientific evidence or an actual
   encounter photograph. Correct errors before approving an image.
5. Check the image at phone size in the catalog, saved card, and discovery view.
   The traits must remain visible with the actual layout and fitting behavior.
   Support per-image framing and avoid crops that remove key features.
6. Record asset identity/version, origin, author or generator, reuse terms,
   source-image credits where applicable, and review status. Keep factual sources
   distinct from image attribution.

## Battery and delivery

Produce artwork during development, not on the user's phone. Bundle static,
right-sized assets for offline use, reuse decoded textures, and avoid animated
illustrations, repeated filtering, or background image generation. Measure asset
memory, decode cost, and scrolling behavior in the mobile build before choosing
final resolutions and formats. Better illustrations must not introduce recurring
network, GPU, or battery work.
